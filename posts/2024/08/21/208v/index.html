208V electricity and some trigonometric identities

2024/08/21

Recently it became necessary for me to prove that my theoretical skills are easily transferable to practical matters. In the process I stumbled on something new: 208V electricity.

Richard Feynman is famously believed to have said: "If you can't explain something in simple terms, you don't understand it." 1 In this post I will explain how 208V comes about 2 and let my readers judge how well I understand it.

We will look at two 3-phase transformer secondary winding configurations: Y (or wye) and high-leg delta, which are mentioned in the top result when I google 208 volt.

For the uninitiated, I recommend this youtube video 3, which provides a comprehensive overview (less the mathematics below) of how 3-phase transformers work and the various configurations.

1.1 208V as the phase-to-phase voltage

This diagram depicts a Y configuration (the three phases are offset by $120^\circ$):

Claim: $? = \sqrt{3}\times120V$.

Using a calculator with [ $\sqrt[2]{x}$ ] key, it's easy to verify that $208 \approx \sqrt{3}\times120$. The question now is where the $\sqrt{3}$ comes from.

What we will prove is the following equality:

$$ V_p\sin(t+60^\circ) - V_p\sin(t-60^\circ) = \sqrt{3}V_p\sin(t+90^\circ) $$(1)

In other words, when we take the difference of the two sine waves with the same amplitude $V_p$ and a phase difference of $120^\circ$, we get a new sine wave with amplitude $\sqrt{3}V_p$. The common factor $V_p$ does not affect the equality so we just need to prove:

$$ \sin(t+60^\circ) - \sin(t-60^\circ) = \sqrt{3}\sin(t+90^\circ) $$(2)

1.2 Some trigonometric identities

1.2.1 $\sin(-\alpha) = -\sin\alpha$

Proof:

This identity can be easily seen using the definition of $\sin\alpha$. When we change the sign of $\alpha$, the right triangle is flipped along the $x$-axis. $y$ changes sign and so does the ratio $y/\sqrt{x^2+y^2}$:

∎

1.2.2 $\cos(-\alpha) = \cos\alpha$

Proof:

$x$ doesn't change sign when the right triangle is flipped, so the ratio stays the same: $x/\sqrt{x^2+(-y)^2} = x/\sqrt{x^2+y^2}$. ∎

1.2.3 $\sin(\alpha + \beta) =$ $\sin\alpha \cos\beta + \cos\alpha \sin\beta$

Proof without words:

∎

1.2.4 $\cos(\alpha + \beta) =$ $\cos\alpha \cos\beta - \sin\alpha \sin\beta$

Proof:

By the same picture. ∎

1.2.5 $\sin(\alpha - \beta) =$ $\sin\alpha \cos\beta - \cos\alpha \sin\beta$

Proof:

By 1.2.3, we have $\sin(\alpha - \beta) = \sin\alpha \cos\beta + \cos\alpha \sin(-\beta)$. By 1.2.1, we can move the minus sign out of $\sin(-\beta)$ and to the middle of the RHS. The resulting RHS is $\sin\alpha \cos\beta - \cos\alpha \sin\beta$ as desired.

∎

1.2.6 $\sin(\alpha+90^\circ) = \cos\alpha$

Proof:

By 1.2.3, we have $\sin(\alpha+90^\circ) = \sin\alpha \cos90^\circ + \cos\alpha \sin90^\circ$. Substitute $\cos90^\circ = 0$ and $\sin90^\circ = 1$ and the RHS simplifies to $\cos\alpha$.

∎

1.2.7 $\cos(\alpha+90^\circ) = -\sin\alpha$

Proof:

Similar. ∎

1.2.8 Digression

Those who have not ventured far enough into mathematics probably have the impression that experts (i.e. their school teachers) often grab rules out of thin air to derive magic-like results. Until the end of the 19th century, the line between math and magic was indeed quite blurred. But then mathematicians realized that rules grabbed out of thin air might not always fit nicely together. Much effort was then devoted to getting the fundamental rules straight, and making sure all mathematics rests solidly on these rules, called "axioms". Those who are interested in getting a taste of such mathematical rigor are encouraged to check out this previous post.

The next subsection, which can be skipped with no loss of continuity, gives a brief overview of how the trig identities we just proved can be derived without appealing to geometric intuition 4. This alternative development, although more convoluted in appearance, actually is more easily traceable to the fundamental axioms.

1.2.9 Alternative development

We start with the power series definition of $e^x$, which I talked about in my very first post 5: $$ e^x := \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{x^n}{n!} $$(3)

Substitute $i\theta$ for $x$, where $i$ is the imaginary unit, we get a complex number 6: $$ e^{i\theta} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(i\theta)^n}{n!} $$(4)

Using $i$'s special property \begin{align*} i^n = \begin{cases} 1, & n \bmod{4} = 0 \\ i, & n \bmod{4} = 1 \\ -1, & n \bmod{4} = 2 \\ -i, & n \bmod{4} = 3 \end{cases} \end{align*} (5) the RHS can be reorganized into a sum of four power series: $$ e^{i\theta} = S_0(\theta) + i S_1(\theta) + (-1)S_2(\theta) + (-i)S_3(\theta) $$(6) where $$ S_k(\theta) := \sum_{\substack{n \in \mathbb{N}\\n \bmod{4} = k}} \frac{\theta^n}{n!} $$(7)

Define $$ \cos\theta = S_0(\theta) - S_2(\theta) $$(8) and $$ \sin\theta = S_1(\theta) - S_3(\theta) $$(9) we obtain the following expression: $$ e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta $$(10)

Now we are ready to harvest the results.

First, because $S_1$ and $S_3$ are sums of odd powers of $\theta$, we have $S_1(-\theta) = -S_1(\theta)$ and $S_3(-\theta) = -S_3(\theta)$, from which we get $\sin(-\theta) = -\sin\theta$. And because $S_0$ and $S_2$ are sums of even powers of $\theta$, we have $S_0(-\theta) = S_0(\theta)$ and $S_2(-\theta) = S_2(\theta)$, from which we get $\cos(-\theta) = \cos\theta$. Thus we have arrived at 1.2.1 and 1.2.2 through a different route.

Now: \begin{align*} &e^{i(\alpha+\beta)} \\ &= e^{i\alpha}e^{i\beta} \\ &= (\cos\alpha + i\sin\alpha)(\cos\beta + i\sin\beta) \\ &= (\cos\alpha \cos\beta - \sin\alpha \sin\beta) \\ & ~~~~ + i(\sin\alpha \cos\beta + \cos\alpha \sin\beta) \end{align*} (11)

Matching the real and imaginary parts with $$ e^{i(\alpha+\beta)} = \cos(\alpha+\beta)+i\sin(\alpha+\beta) $$(12) we get 1.2.3: $$\sin(\alpha + \beta) = \sin\alpha \cos\beta + \cos\alpha \sin\beta$$(13) and 1.2.4: $$\cos(\alpha + \beta) = \cos\alpha \cos\beta - \sin\alpha \sin\beta$$(14).

1.3 Proof of (2)

Using the trig identities above, we can derive (2) as: \begin{align*} & \sin(t+60^\circ) - \sin(t-60^\circ) \\ &= (\sin t \cos 60^\circ + \cos t \sin 60^\circ) \\ & ~~~~ -(\sin t \cos 60^\circ - \cos t \sin 60^\circ) \\ &= 2 \cos t \sin 60^\circ \\ &= 2 \cdot \frac{\sqrt3}{2} \cos t \\ &= \sqrt3 \sin(t+90^\circ) \end{align*} (15)

1.4 Remarks

The degrees in (2) are chosen for convenience. We can vary the phases (which corresponds to sliding the sine waves left and right), but as long as the LHS phase difference is $120^\circ$, the $\sqrt{3}$ on the RHS persists: \begin{align*} &\sin(t+\phi+60^\circ) - \sin(t+\phi-60^\circ) \\ &= \sqrt3 \sin(t+\phi+90^\circ) \end{align*} (16)

Also, note that it seems to be the convention that in the context of AC, when "voltage" appears without a modifier, it refers to the RMS voltage. See for example this list, which states: "All voltages are root mean square voltages".

There is already a section devoted to the math of RMS of sine waves, so I am not repeating the effort here. The gist is that $V_p = \sqrt{2} V_{rms}$. So when we multiply $V_p$ by $\sqrt{3}$ we also multiply $V_{rms}$ by $\sqrt{3}$ and vice versa.

Wikipedia already has a proof. Armed with the trig identities above, we can now rewrite it using the more familiar sines and cosines: \begin{align*} &2\sin(t+120^\circ) + \sin t \\ &= 2\sin(t+90^\circ+30^\circ) + \sin t \\ &= 2\sin(t+90^\circ)\cos30^\circ \\ & ~~~~ + 2\cos(t+90^\circ)\sin30^\circ + \sin t \\ &= 2 \cdot \frac{\sqrt3}{2}\sin(t+90^\circ) \\ & ~~~~ + 2 \cdot \frac{1}{2}\cos(t+90^\circ) + \sin t \\ &= \sqrt3 \sin(t+90^\circ) - \sin t + \sin t \\ &= \sqrt3 \sin(t+90^\circ) \end{align*} (18)
